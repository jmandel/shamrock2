How a Shamrock table stays one table

One model for keeping every phone at a table in agreement, whatever the network, the timing or the people do. Everything about it is in this one document, and the code follows it.

The cases it must handle

Everything odd that can happen at a table falls into four general cases. The model treats each by how the histories are related, never by the story that produced it. The examples we discussed are points drawn from these cases, and the design must not be fitted to any one of them.

CaseHow the histories relateExamples
One table, in orderEach round follows the lastDeal, write, guess, next round.
One table, divergedTwo histories with a common pastA split network heals. Two people deal or press Proceed at the same moment. A phone offline for three rounds comes back. A reload restores a stale save.
Two tables, one roomTwo histories with no common pastTwo groups used the same room name without meeting, or one group split up and played on separately, then they meet.
Comings and goingsWho is connected changesJoining late, reloading, closing a tab mid-drag, everyone leaving and someone coming back, being alone, the signaling relay going down.

The second and third rows are the important distinction. Divergent histories share a past, so merging them is like resolving a fork: pick a branch to continue and keep the other as history. Separate histories share nothing, so a merge is really two groups landing in one room. Comparing "their round 3" with "our round 2" is arbitrary, and the right answer belongs to the people involved.

Seven principles

  1. The room name is the address, and a room holds one live table. Anyone who opens the link is at that table.
  2. The shared table is small and current: the seats plus the current round. Nothing else syncs. A newcomer only needs what is happening now, never the past.
  3. Merging can't depend on order. Every merge rule is commutative, associative and idempotent, so devices agree whatever order messages arrive in, and nothing ever needs undoing.
  4. History is personal. Each device keeps its own copy of every round it took part in. Work is never lost from the device that did it, even when the table moves on without it.
  5. Every change of round is explained, the same way whatever caused it. The explanation comes from the round's own header.
  6. Presence is not state. Who is here, pointers, and "someone is dragging this card" live in short-lived messages that end when a device leaves. They can never go stale in the table.
  7. The table heals itself. Full syncs, a fingerprint in every presence ping and each device's own save repair it without anyone noticing.

  • Seats are keyed by name, so choosing a name takes that seat. A seat is never deleted, only marked inactive (by Remove, or by a New game for players who aren't there). A device that is here keeps its own seat active.
  • The header says which round this is and where it came from. table answers "is this my table?", parent answers "is this the next round, or a fork?", and dealer says who did it. The deck shuffle is derived from the table and the epoch, so the separate deck_seed field goes new.
  • The body merges register by register, as today. Clue versions are all kept, so "what counted when guessing started" is well defined even when an edit races Proceed. A change that belongs to a round other than the current one is ignored by the table. It isn't lost: see History is personal.

Starting apart from one invite

A group invite supplies a common starting point before any devices connect. Its versioned ?i= payload names a fresh room, one round identity and seed, and an ordered roster of 2–12 players. The core reconstructs the same round and seats on every device. Names are unique under the existing seat identity rule; reserving them does not make those players appear online.

The first visit asks Who are you?, with a full-name button for each player. It does not auto-select a name remembered from a different table. Choosing a currently occupied name requires another confirmation to switch devices. Each person can write and submit while alone; guessing waits for the reserved roster, with the existing explicit Start anyway override.

A subset can start guessing while reserved writers finish later. Those writers see their own clover and can switch between Guess with group and Write my clover. Submitting adds their clover to the same round. Selection names a writer, not an index into the growing clover list, so existing cards, checks, and the current board stay put. After the available clovers finish, a late submission reopens guessing; explicitly dealing the next round ends this opportunity.

The invite is merged before the local save and before connecting. It carries ordinary round and seat operations, so replaying it cannot undo later clues, a later round or an explicit New game. The browser keeps the invite with its saved table links. Compatible saves never expire, for invited and ordinary tables alike. The invite carries the initial deal only: later work is kept locally until peers connect and exchange it. No relay stores the game. Later rounds use the usual roster of players present when dealt.

Which round is current

When two rounds meet, one fixed order decides, identically on every device:

  1. 1Higher epoch. A New game beats everything from before it.
  2. 2Higher round number. The game further along carries on.
  3. 3More players dealt in. A lone device's deal can't beat a deal for the whole table.
  4. 4Older. An established deal beats a later one.

The winner becomes current everywhere. On a device whose round lost, the round's state is cleared; the seats are untouched. This order is the mechanism. It is meaningful for one table, and merely deterministic for two, which is why two tables also get a human choice (Moving your group).

How each part merges

Every change carries its round, and every part of the table has a rule for combining two versions that doesn't depend on which arrived first. So any set of changes, delivered in any order and any number of times, gives one table. The rule: a field that goes into the shared table says how it merges, and core/tests/merge.rs covers it. That test applies random changes, from several competing rounds, in shuffled orders with duplicates, and as two halves that meet. It requires one result, and that each change reports "changed" exactly when it changed something. Stamps are unique (a device's clock and its id), so "newest" is never a tie.

PartMerges by
Seats, per playerNewest stamp. They belong to the table, so any round's change counts.
Moves, per tableNewest stamp, from any round.
The current roundHighest rank (see Which round is current). Taking a new round clears the old round's clues, cards, checks and start, and changes for any other round are ignored.
Clues, per writerEvery version is kept, by stamp; the latest counts.
Start of guessingNewest stamp.
Cards, per clover and cardNewest stamp.
Checks, per cloverMost attempts, then newest stamp. The placements a check rejected are combined from both versions, so two checks made at once both count. The test also requires that no rejection is ever lost.
Which clover is upNewest stamp, selecting a writer identity (0 means the available clovers are finished). Protocol 4 adds Focus; legacy Next records from protocol 3 are still readable and are pinned to a writer on resume.

Some things don't merge, by design: who is here (from live connections), and anything one device keeps for itself (Past games, What happened, saves). A change for a round the table has moved past isn't merged either. It goes to Past games on the devices that were in that round (History is personal). Getting every change to every device is the protocol's job, not the merge's: see How the table heals itself.

Is this a CRDT?

Mostly, yes. The shared table is a state-based CRDT (a conflict-free replicated data type): replicas combine their states with a merge that's commutative, associative and idempotent, so they agree once each has seen everything. The changes sent over the wire are small pieces of that state (a "delta-state" CRDT), and a full sync is the whole state, so the same merge serves both. Every part is a standard CRDT:

PartStandard CRDT it is
Seats, moves, cards, start, which clover is upLast-writer-wins registers, keyed in maps (an LWW map). Stamps are Lamport clocks with the device id to break ties, so "latest" respects cause and effect: a change made after seeing another always wins over it.
Clue versionsA grow-only set of versions. Before Start, read the latest. At Start, freeze the latest pre-Start version if it was ready. Otherwise freeze the first ready version stamped after Start; until then, show the latest draft. The same merged versions always produce the same clover, regardless of arrival order.
A checkA pair: the result keeps the most attempts, then the latest stamp (an LWW register), and the rejected placements are a grow-only set (merged by union).
The current round with everything in itA lexicographic pair: the higher-ranked round wins outright, and only equal rounds merge their contents. Because rank is a total order, this is itself a lattice, and it's how a new round or New game resets the table without tombstones: the old round's contents are simply outranked.

core/tests/merge.rs checks the laws that make it a CRDT, on the real code: any order, any number of repeats, and two halves merged either way give one table.

Where it isn't a textbook CRDT, on purpose

  • Last writer wins means a lost write. Two players dragging the same card at once: one drag wins, the other's is overwritten. A general CRDT library (Automerge, Yjs) would do the same for a single value. For text it would merge character by character, but a clue has one writer and a few words, so a whole-value register fits.
  • Changes to a round the table has left are dropped from the table. The merge is still sound, since they're outranked, but players would lose work. So the page keeps them outside the CRDT: in Past games on the devices that were in that round (History is personal). That's a deliberate application-level layer, not part of the shared state.
  • Delivery isn't guaranteed by the transport. CRDT convergence assumes every update eventually reaches every replica. A browser mesh with no server drops whatever was in flight when a link closes. The usual remedy for state-based CRDTs supplies it: a full state exchange whenever a link opens, plus table checksums in presence pings that trigger a resync when two devices differ (How the table heals itself). The merge makes those resends harmless. The resync makes them happen.
  • Some state isn't replicated at all. Who is here, live pointers and cards being held are soft state with time-outs, rebuilt from live connections. They aren't a CRDT and don't need to be: there's nothing to agree on once someone has gone.
  • Decisions are made from state, then written as changes. "Everyone has submitted, so guessing starts" is computed from the merged table and recorded as a Start. If two devices decide at once, the register keeps one. Rules like this one are why the table is a small fixed schema, not a general document.

Why not a CRDT library

Automerge or Yjs would give the registers and maps for free, but not the round rule. Its "a higher round replaces everything" order, with rejection sets inside, would still be custom code on top. And either would add a dependency, and its own document format, for a schema of eight fields. The hand-written version stays small (a table saves in a few hundred bytes), stays bounded (each round resets the table, so nothing accumulates over an evening), and is checked directly by the merge test and the simulator.

Explaining any change new

Whenever the round a device is in is replaced by one it didn't deal, the device compares the new round's header with the one it had. One classifier covers every cause, and produces one notice. The person who caused the change sees nothing.

The table moved on

r2 · yours→r3 · parent r2
The new round's parent is your round. "Cy dealt round 3." Or, if you weren't dealt in: "Cy dealt round 3; you're in next round."

Same table, different branch

r2→r3 · yours
r2→r3 · Ben's, older
Same table and epoch, but the parent isn't yours: you missed rounds, or two deals crossed. "Ben dealt round 3 while you were away." or "Ben dealt at the same moment; continuing Ben's."

A New game

epoch 0 · r4 · yours→epoch 1 · lobby
Same table, higher epoch. "Ana started a new game."

Another table in this room

table A · r1 · yours⇢table B · r2
A different table id: no shared past at all. "Cy and Dee have also been playing at this table." The default is to play together, and the notice offers Move us to a new table (below).

Arriving

You had no table yet: a first visit, or no compatible save. No notice; you just see the table.

Whenever the round you're leaving had anything in it, the notice ends with "Your round is in Past games."

History is personal new

The rule: when the round a device is in stops being current, for any reason, the device writes its own copy of that round to Past games.

Reasons include finishing the round, a teammate dealing again, losing a crossed deal, a New game, or another table winning. One rule replaces today's two separate paths (round-end logging, and the special "set aside" event).

  • What's kept: whatever this device had. That means clues (drafts included), card placements and guesses.
  • How it's labelled: a finished round is recorded as finished. Anything else is marked unfinished, with each clover marked done, guessed so far, or not guessed.
  • Replay: Play these again offers only clovers with all four clues. Half-written clovers can be read but not replayed.
  • Limit: the last 60 rounds per device exists.

Late edits to a round that was just replaced

Each device keeps the last round it was pushed out of, alive in the background, until its round changes again (one table's worth of memory). Any change that arrives for that round merges into it rather than being ignored. The device's Past games copy is updated, and so is what a Move would carry. A teammate's last clue edit that crossed the switch in flight still lands where it belongs.

What this deliberately doesn't do: share old rounds with the table. A newcomer has no use for rounds from before they arrived, and the people who care about a replaced round were in it, so their devices already hold it. Older rounds than the last one are only on the devices that played them.

Moving your group new

When two tables meet in one room, playing together is the default, and it needs nothing from anyone. But the two groups may be strangers, or may simply want their own games back. The notice on the outranked side offers Move us to a new table.

  1. One tap, by anyone in the group. Their device picks a fresh room name and opens it, seeded with its copy of the group's table: the round they were in and their seats. They arrive already seated, mid-round, exactly where they were.
  2. The table remembers the move. A small shared record goes into the old room: the moved group's table id → the new room name. It's last-writer-wins and a few bytes long. Because it's in the room's table, every device of that group sees it, whenever it next connects: now, after a reload, or later from its save (saves do not expire).
  3. The rest of the group gets one tap to follow. Their notice reads "Ana moved your group to #quiet-heron-dell. Go there?" Following opens the new room seeded with their own copy too. Copies of the same round from the same table merge, so everyone's drafts arrive.
  4. Nobody is moved without choosing. The other group isn't affected at all and keeps the room. A group member who ignores the prompt simply stays at the merged table.

Who is "your group"

Nobody keeps a member list. A group is a table id, and each device knows its own from its history: it's the table of the round it was pushed out of when the tables met. The move record is keyed by that id ("table A moved to #quiet-heron-dell"), and a device is offered the move only if it came from table A.

  • The other group's devices came from the winning table, so they're never offered it.
  • Anyone who arrived after the merge has only seen the merged table, so they aren't offered it either.
  • A device that reopens later from a save of table A is offered it then.
  • Two players with the same name in both groups share one seat at the merged table, but their devices have different histories. Each is offered only its own group's move.
  • A group member on a new phone, with no history, isn't offered it. The group can send them the link.

Starting a fresh table any time

The ⋯ menu has Start a fresh table with this group for a table that just wants to split off. At an ordinary table "the group" would be everyone, so this moves nobody automatically. It opens the new room seeded with this device's copy of the table and gives you its link to send to whoever you choose.

How the table heals itself exists

MechanismWhat it covers
A full sync (seats plus the current round) every time a connection opensAnyone joining or reconnecting
A table fingerprint in every Hello. If a peer's differs twice in a row with neither side changing, both resync, at most every 5 sDevices that drifted apart while staying connected, such as a change that missed one player and whose sender then left
Each device saves the table every 2 s and on leaving, and restores compatible saves without an age limitEvery holder reloading or closing; one person coming back alone, even months later
A saved name rejoins 1.5 s after the page loadsReloads

A restored save is not trusted. It merges like any other sync and is classified like any other round, so a stale save either catches up quietly or is recognised as another table.

Upgrades new

A change to the message format means a phone still on the old page can't read a new phone's messages. Without a guard they would silently drift apart. So:

  • Every Hello carries a protocol version.
  • A device that hears a newer one shows "A newer version of Shamrock is at this table. Reload to keep playing." and stops sending table changes until it reloads.
  • A save is restored only if its version matches the page. An older save starts fresh and syncs from whoever is there.

Worked examples

Two pairs, one room

  1. Ana and Ben deal round 1 at family-night. Ana writes one clue, then they put their phones away. Each phone saves the table.
  2. Later, Cy and Dee open family-night on their own phones. There's no table there for them, so their first deal starts a new one (a different table id), and they play into round 2.
  3. All four come back the same evening. Each phone restores its own save, then they connect and do full syncs.
  4. Seats merge: four players at one table. Rounds rank: Cy and Dee's round 2 beats round 1, so it becomes current everywhere.
  5. Ana's and Ben's phones see a different table id. They show "Cy and Dee have also been playing at this table. You're in next round. Your round is in Past games." and write their round 1 (with Ana's clue) to Past games, marked unfinished.
  6. Cy and Dee lost nothing, so they see nothing. The next deal includes all four.
  7. Or Ana taps Move us to a new table. She lands at quiet-heron-dell, back in round 1 with her clue. Ben's phone shows "Ana moved your group to #quiet-heron-dell. Go there?", and one tap takes him there with his own drafts too. Cy and Dee carry on at family-night, unaffected.

Two deals cross during a network split

  1. Four players are in round 2, and the network splits two and two. Each side presses Deal new cards.
  2. When it heals, both round 3s have the same table, epoch, number and player count, so the older deal wins.
  3. The losing side sees "Ben dealt at the same moment; continuing Ben's. Your round is in Past games." Their drafts are on their own phones.

A reload with a stale save

  1. Dee's phone was offline through rounds 3 and 4, and she reloads. Her save says round 2.
  2. She reconnects. Round 4 has the same table and outranks her round. Its parent isn't her round, but her round 2 had finished, so there is nothing to keep.
  3. She catches up quietly, or sees at most "Cy dealt round 4 while you were away."

Bounds over a long evening

  • The shared table: the seats plus one round. The only growth inside a round is clue versions, about 60–100 bytes per edit, so a chatty six-player round is about 15 KB. It never accumulates across rounds, so syncs, fingerprints and saves stay that size however long a table plays. No compaction is needed.
  • Seats: one per name ever used at the table, at about 30 bytes each. Even a room used by a hundred different names adds 3 KB. Rather than prune them, which a merge would undo, the size is measured: the simulator checks that the table stays under a fixed budget (50 KB) through its longest games.
  • Move records: one per group that ever moved out of a room, a few bytes each.
  • The last replaced round kept for late edits: at most one table per device, in memory, until the round changes again.
  • Past games: 60 rounds per device.

Guarantees and how they're checked

Network simulator

core/tests/network.rs: 300 games of everyday chaos on every test run, plus 40 games for each of eight story families (a long split, two tables meeting, mass reload, a New game in a split, edits crossing round changes, churn, a long session, version skew) and a move story, each with a coverage rule that its situation actually occurred. Links drop, the network splits and heals, phones go offline, reload or close, and players act at random. After everything heals, each game must show:

  • one state on every device;
  • nobody here without their seat;
  • no device's table ever back to less progress;
  • no hold outliving its hand;
  • a round that can still be played to its end;
  • new every round a device was in is in its Past games once replaced;
  • new every change a device didn't cause gives exactly one notice, correctly classified (the simulator knows the true cause);
  • new a late edit to a just-replaced round reaches every device that was in it, in Past games;
  • new a moved group, once everyone follows, has one table at the new room with every member's work, and the old room is untouched;
  • new the shared table stays under its size budget;
  • new only the moved group's devices are offered the move.

Browser fault scenarios

harness/invites.mjs exercises one four-player invite through separate browser profiles: everyone writes alone, month-old saves reopen, and all clovers converge over real WebRTC. It also checks the twelve-player phone picker, occupied-seat confirmation, copied and saved links, malformed invites, ordinary saves a year old, explicit resets and failed-save retries. Native tests cover every group size from 2 to 12; a 40-run network story requires independent submissions, reloads, partitions and all clovers reaching completion.

harness/faults.mjs: real browsers, one profile per player, faults injected into the page and the relay:

  • a join storm;
  • a phone cut off with its connections up;
  • a 600 ms lag;
  • a reload mid-writing;
  • everyone leaving and coming back;
  • the relay going down;
  • a phone dying mid-drag;
  • being alone;
  • two groups meeting.

new Each scenario also checks the notice and the Past games entry. A new scenario moves a group: one taps Move, another follows from the prompt, a third reopens the old room later from its save and is offered the move. It checks both rooms afterwards, and that the other group was never offered it. Two more check that round changes appear under What happened, and that a page on an older protocol version is told to reload rather than drifting.

What changes from today

Goes away

  • The "set aside" event and its deck-shuffle test
  • The deck_seed field (derived from table and epoch instead)
  • Round-end logging as a separate path

Comes in

  • table, parent and dealer in the round header
  • One classifier and one notice for every round change
  • One rule: a round that stops being current goes to Past games
  • Past games marks unfinished clovers; only complete ones can be replayed
  • The last replaced round kept briefly, so late edits land in it
  • Move us to a new table, with a move record in the room and a prompt to follow
  • A size budget for the table, checked by the simulator
  • What happened at this table in the ⋯ menu
  • A protocol version in Hello and in saves
Built as described: every check above passes, in the native tests (core/tests), the network simulator (core/tests/network.rs) and the browser fault scenarios (harness/faults.mjs).
